    03 / FROM DATA TO VALUE

    An agent works with the context and tools your system supplies. Give it reliable data, explicit business meaning, relevant memory, and a clear path to ask for help. Then measure whether the whole process improves the business.

    Data foundationsDatabase semanticsRetrieval & memoryBusiness knowledgeHuman oversightBusiness valueAgents & skillsEvaluation
    01 Make data fit for the decision

    A model cannot reliably repair conflicting customer IDs, missing records, or an undefined revenue metric. Start with the business question and identify the authoritative sources needed to answer it.

    Establish the foundation

    • Assign a data owner and a steward who can resolve quality issues.
    • Define entities, stable identifiers, units, timestamps, and the grain: what one row represents.
    • Check completeness, uniqueness, valid relationships, freshness, and coverage for the target task.
    • Document lineage from source to transformations to the records supplied to the agent.
    • Enforce access and privacy rules in the data service; record versions and refresh schedules.

    Example: customer renewal

    Link CRM accounts, contracts, and invoices using an approved customer ID. Mark whether invoices are paid, overdue, or disputed. A stale contract or a duplicated account can change the recommendation.

    Failure behavior: If a required source fails its quality or freshness check, show the missing evidence and route the decision to the owner.

    Make it reviewable: A source inventory, a data contract, ownership, quality checks, lineage, access rules, and an agreed freshness target. Better foundations improve reliability; they do not guarantee correct model output.

    02 Teach the meaning of database tables and metrics

    A schema tells an agent which columns exist. A semantic layer tells it what those columns mean for the business. Valid SQL can still answer the wrong question.

    Give database access a business contract

    • Describe tables, column meanings, primary and foreign keys, allowed join paths, and one-to-many relationships.
    • Define metrics, aggregation rules, exclusions, fiscal periods, time zones, currencies, and effective dates.
    • Map business phrases to approved measures: “sales” may mean booked orders, invoiced sales, or recognized revenue.
    • Prefer approved views or parameterized metric tools. If generating SQL, validate its structure, permissions, resource limits, and business semantics before execution.
    • Use a read-only execution identity with row and column access controls. A prompt instruction is not an access boundary.

    The join trap

    Order A totals $100 and has two items. Order B totals $200 and has two items. Summing order totals after joining to items gives $600. The correct order total is $300.

    Choose the grain before aggregating. Do not use SUM(DISTINCT total) as a general fix: different orders can have the same total.

    Business question→Approved metric + time window→Validated query + permissions→Result + provenance
    -- Fictional metric: paid order value in USD, by order date.
-- Each row in orders represents one order; returns excluded from this metric.
SELECT SUM(total_usd) AS paid_order_value_usd
FROM orders
WHERE status = 'paid'
  AND ordered_at >= :period_start
  AND ordered_at < :period_end;

    Make it reviewable: A versioned glossary and metric catalog, approved joins and views, example questions with expected queries, and tests on both SQL results and business interpretation.

    03 Use short-term and long-term memory deliberately

    Model weights, conversation history, and an external memory store are different things. An agent only uses the relevant information actually supplied to its current run. Storing more text does not ensure useful recall.

    Short-term: this task

    Keep the current question, confirmed constraints, recent tool results, unresolved questions, and workflow state. Scope it to the session and user.

    Summarize long conversations within a context budget, preserving source references and uncertainty. Never let a summary erase an approval requirement.

    Long-term: selected, reusable records

    Store approved preferences, verified facts, and reusable lessons only when there is a legitimate purpose. Include scope, provenance, owner, sensitivity, version, and expiry.

    Control writes, correction, retention, and deletion. Do not automatically promote an agent’s guess or a raw conversation into trusted knowledge.

    Task + identity→Authorized sources + relevant memories→Rank, deduplicate, check freshness→Bounded context + citations
    • Route structured questions to metric tools; retrieve prose from approved documents. Use keyword and vector search together when tests show they improve recall.
    • Filter by access, entity, business unit, effective date, and task scope before selecting context. Recheck permission changes when reading stored memories.
    • Rerank useful candidates, remove duplicates, and reserve context for evidence rather than filling the window with unrelated history.
    • Define conflict rules: the current authoritative policy or live system record should override an old memory. Flag unresolved conflicts.

    Example: Remembering that a customer prefers email can help a service agent. Remembering last month’s balance should never replace a fresh billing query. Test retrieval and memory for stale facts, cross-user leakage, incorrect writes, and successful correction or deletion.

    04 Govern business knowledge as a product

    Business knowledge includes definitions, operating procedures, decision rules, exceptions, and the reasons behind them. Source it from policy owners, approved procedures, governed metrics, and documented decisions. Capture expert knowledge through review before publishing it for agents.

    A knowledge product needs

    • A named owner, intended audience, permitted uses, and a way to request corrections.
    • Source provenance, approval status, version, effective dates, review dates, and a refresh commitment.
    • Definitions of authority and scope: which rule applies to which product, region, or customer group?
    • A conflict-resolution path, change review, deprecation, and notification when a rule changes.
    • Examples and evaluation cases that show both the rule and its exceptions.

    Example: refund guidance

    A finance policy owner approves the eligibility rule. Regional exceptions carry scope and dates. The agent retrieves the applicable version and cites it; a backend enforces the rule before action.

    A revoked policy must be removed from retrieval caches and corrected or expired in related memories.

    Control boundary: Retrieved documents are evidence, not instructions allowed to rewrite agent rules. Enforce mandatory policy in code where feasible. Audit changes to knowledge alongside changes to data products.

    05 Embed people in the workflow from the start

    Decide who can approve, correct, pause, and recover the process while designing it. “A human can look at it later” is not an operating model.

    Human sets scope→Agent gathers evidence→Exceptions go to owner→Reviewer approves action→Backend rechecks + logs
    • At intake: Confirm intent, scope, and missing facts for consequential or ambiguous requests.
    • During work: Escalate contradictory sources, missing evidence, policy exceptions, and failed tool calls using observable triggers. Do not rely only on the model’s self-reported confidence.
    • Before execution: Show the exact action, parameters, evidence, uncertainty, and expected consequence to an authorized reviewer. Approval must bind to that proposal.
    • After execution: Capture receipts and corrections; assign incident handling, a pause switch, and a manual fallback.

    Make it workable: Name the reviewer, response-time target, queue capacity, and timeout behavior. If approval expires or no reviewer responds, hold or escalate; silence is not approval. Give reviewers authority and time to challenge the result, and measure whether reviews catch errors.

    06 Capture value with a measurable business case

    Start with a decision or process that needs improvement. Compare the current workflow, deterministic automation, and an AI solution. Select AI when the pilot supports better outcomes at acceptable cost and risk.

    1. Define the owner, eligible population, baseline, unit, measurement source, and time horizon.
    2. Select an outcome: net handling time, rework, resolution quality, conversion, or a measured reduction in loss. Track quality alongside speed.
    3. Run a pilot against a comparable baseline. Use a controlled comparison where feasible and record changes in case mix, staffing, and adoption.
    4. Include integration, data preparation, model use, retrieval, review, monitoring, support, and change management costs.
    5. Agree on scale, revise, or stop criteria before the pilot. Measure realized value after rollout.

    Fictional monthly service pilot

    1,000 eligible cases × 80% adoption × 4 minutes saved ÷ 60 = 53.3 hours of capacity. Handling time falls from 12 to 8 minutes, including human review and rework.

    At $30/hour, the capacity value is $1,600. If only 50% converts to measurable benefit, realized benefit is $800. With $700 in allocated monthly total costs, net benefit is $100 and ROI is 14.3%.

    Net benefit = realized benefit − total cost
    ROI = net benefit / total cost × 100%

    Time freed is not automatically cash saved. Record how capacity is used; avoid counting the same benefit under both time saved and throughput. These figures are illustrative assumptions, not a forecast.

    Make it reviewable: A value scorecard with baseline, pilot result, adoption, quality, realized benefit, full costs, owner, and the decision to scale or stop.

    07 Extend capabilities through clear agent and skill contracts

    Start with one orchestrator and a small set of well-defined capabilities. Add specialist agents when a bounded responsibility or independently testable workflow earns the added coordination cost.

    OrchestratorOwns task state, routing, budgets, and escalation
    Data specialistApproved metric tools
    Knowledge specialistScoped document retrieval
    Review capabilityChecks evidence and proposals

    Each returns structured results with source IDs, versions, status, and limitations. Policy and human approval still gate execution.

    Define each capability

    • Tool: A callable operation with typed input, output, permissions, side effects, and error behavior.
    • Skill: A reusable, versioned procedure that specifies when to apply it and which tools or knowledge it needs.
    • Agent: A bounded role that uses a model and tools to pursue a delegated task. Define what it may decide and what it must return.

    Extend without losing control

    Register a capability by name, version, schema, owner, allowed tools, and evaluation suite. Pin versions and test before enabling it.

    Limit delegation depth, time, cost, retries, and tool scope. Pass only needed context. Delegation never grants broader permissions.

    Coordination rule: Keep an explicit shared task state, trace handoffs, validate returned results, and stop loops or duplicate actions. Two agents agreeing is not independent proof. Measure the added capability against a single-agent baseline.

    08 Evaluate the whole path from evidence to outcome

    Define correctness for the task before releasing an agent. Build a held-out set from approved, representative cases plus known failures and adversarial inputs. Domain experts provide expected results or a rubric; protect sensitive records and prevent test leakage.

    Evaluate each layer, then test the complete workflow
    LayerWhat to checkExample measure
    Data & semanticsFreshness, grain, correct definitions and joinsMetric results match approved reference queries
    Retrieval & memoryRelevant, authorized, current evidence; isolated memoryRecall@k on labeled evidence; zero observed cross-user leakage in the test set
    AnswerCorrectness, source support, complete material claimsExpert-scored accuracy and citation support
    Tools & actionsCorrect arguments, approval, eligibility, duplicate preventionAuthorized task success; blocked prohibited-action cases
    Failure handlingMissing data, conflicting policies, outages, injectionAppropriate abstention, escalation, and recovery
    Business operationQuality, latency, cost, adoption, reviewer workloadRealized benefit against the baseline

    Interpret metrics carefully: Recall@k is the share of labeled relevant items found among the top k retrieved results. It does not measure answer correctness. An LLM judge can help triage, but calibrate its rubric against expert reviews and investigate disagreements.

    • Report results by business unit, risk, language, and task type. Repeat runs where outputs vary and show sample size and uncertainty.
    • Set risk-specific acceptance thresholds before testing. Block release on designated critical failures; a good average cannot cancel them out.
    • Version the model, prompt, data, semantic definitions, index, memory policy, tools, and skills. Re-run affected evaluations after changes.
    • Use shadow mode or a limited pilot, monitor corrections and incidents, and define rollback triggers. Feed verified failures into regression cases.

    The practical limit: Passing evaluations provides evidence for a defined scope; it cannot guarantee every future answer. Keep runtime validation, source checks, and human escalation in place.

    START WITH A SMALL SLICE

    Choose one business question, one approved source, one accountable owner, and a measurable result. Prove the checks before adding autonomy.

    The math behind LLMs & reasoning

    05 / UNDERSTAND THE ENGINE

    Start with the intuition, then open each topic for the math and a business example. These concepts describe common autoregressive Transformer models; implementation details vary.

    Text → tokens→ vectors + position→ Transformer layers→ next-token probabilities→ choose a token & repeat
    01 Tokens & conditional probability

    An LLM generates a sequence one token at a time. Tokens can be words, parts of words, or punctuation. Each choice depends on the available context and previously generated tokens.

    P(x₁, …, xₙ) = ∏ₜ P(xₜ | x₁, …, xₜ₋₁)

    Read it: The probability of a sequence is the product of its next-token probabilities. The ∏ symbol means “multiply,” and | means “given.”

    Example: After “The invoice total is,” the model predicts a continuation. A likely continuation can still contain an incorrect amount.

    Business implication: token probability measures likelihood under the model, not whether a statement is true.

    02 Embeddings & vector similarity

    Token embeddings map token IDs to learned lists of numbers called vectors. Position information helps represent order. Retrieval systems often use a separate embedding model to represent whole questions and passages.

    cosine(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)

    Read it: Multiply corresponding coordinates and add them for the dot product a · b. Divide by the two vector lengths to compare direction.

    Example: For a = [1, 0] and b = [1, 1], similarity is 1 / √2 ≈ 0.707. This is a geometric score, not a 70.7% chance that a document is correct.

    Business implication: related passages may still be outdated, unauthorized, or irrelevant to the exact question.

    03 Attention & context

    Attention mixes information across token positions. Queries (Q) and keys (K) produce matching scores; values (V) carry the information being combined.

    Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dₖ) V

    Read it: Compare queries with keys, scale by the square root of key dimension dₖ, normalize the scores, and combine values. A causal mask blocks future tokens during prediction.

    Example: In “The contract expires in June. Renew it,” context helps connect “it” to the contract.

    Business implication: attention weights are not a reliable explanation of why an answer is correct.

    04 Layers & learned transformations

    A Transformer repeats attention and feed-forward transformations, with residual connections and normalization. A simplified feed-forward calculation is:

    FFN(h) = W₂ φ(W₁h + b₁) + b₂

    Read it: Learned matrices W transform the input vector h, biases b shift it, and a nonlinear function φ lets layers represent more than a single linear mapping.

    Example: Successive layers transform the representation of a sentence before the output layer scores possible next tokens.

    Business implication: model weights encode learned patterns; they are not a searchable ledger of verified business facts.

    05 Training loss & gradient descent

    During next-token pretraining, the model learns from known continuations. Cross-entropy penalizes assigning a low probability to the actual next token. Gradient descent adjusts the parameters.

    L = −(1/N) ∑ₜ log Pθ(xₜ | x<ₜ)
    θ ← θ − η ∇θL

    Read it: Average the negative log probabilities over N training targets. θ represents learned parameters, η is the learning rate, and ∇θL points toward increasing loss.

    Example: Giving the actual token probability 0.8 produces a smaller penalty than giving it 0.2. Later training may use other objectives to improve instruction following or reasoning.

    Business implication: lower prediction loss does not by itself establish fitness for your business task. Test task outcomes separately.

    06 Softmax, temperature & sampling

    The model outputs scores called logits. Softmax converts scores to a probability distribution; decoding chooses the next token.

    pᵢ = exp(zᵢ / T) / ∑ⱼ exp(zⱼ / T), T > 0

    Read it: Exponentiate each score z after dividing by temperature T, then divide by the total. The probabilities sum to one.

    Example: With two logits [2, 1], probabilities are approximately [0.731, 0.269] at T = 1 and [0.881, 0.119] at T = 0.5. Lower temperature concentrates the distribution.

    Business implication: less variation does not guarantee more factual accuracy. Deterministic decoding can repeatedly return the same mistake.

    07 Reasoning as intermediate computation

    Generating intermediate steps can help a model decompose a problem and use earlier results. Research on chain-of-thought prompting found improvements on several reasoning benchmarks; this is an empirical technique, not a guarantee of sound logic.

    Given: 120 units × $25
    Subtotal: $3,000
    After 10% discount: $3,000 × 0.90 = $2,700

    Read it: Each intermediate result becomes an input to the next operation. In a business system, send arithmetic to a calculator and retain inputs and checked results.

    Limits: A fluent explanation can contain an invalid step or rationalize a wrong conclusion. A visible explanation is not a complete or necessarily faithful account of internal computation.

    Business implication: request a concise justification, evidence, assumptions, and verifiable results; do not treat length as proof of reasoning quality.

    08 Verification, multiple attempts & error

    Self-consistency samples multiple solution paths and selects a common answer. It improved results in the cited study, but agreement is not independent verification: models can share the same mistaken assumption.

    Illustration: P(all 10 steps correct) = 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 0.599

    Read it: If ten steps each had 95% accuracy and their errors were independent, all ten would be correct about 60% of the time. Real reasoning errors can be dependent; this is a teaching illustration, not an LLM accuracy estimate.

    Example: Three matching refund recommendations still fail if they all use an expired policy. Check against the current policy and an authorized transaction system.

    Business implication: use independent checks—source validation, calculations, rule engines, and human review—at consequential steps.

    09 Reasoning training & compute budgets

    Some reasoning models receive additional training that rewards successful solutions. At inference time, they may spend more computation generating intermediate tokens or exploring alternatives.

    J(θ) = E[ R(solution) ]

    Read it: A simplified reinforcement learning objective is to increase expected reward R under the model’s generated solutions. Practical algorithms add constraints and other terms; a reward must be designed and checked.

    Example: A math task can reward answers verified by a checker. An ambiguous business recommendation rarely has such a clean correctness signal.

    Business implication: more reasoning effort can trade higher cost and latency for better task performance, but improvement is not guaranteed. Measure accuracy, cost, and response time on your own cases.
