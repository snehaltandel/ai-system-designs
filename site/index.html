The math behind LLMs & reasoning

03 / UNDERSTAND THE ENGINE

Start with the intuition, then open each topic for the math and a business example. These concepts describe common autoregressive Transformer models; implementation details vary.

Text → tokens→ vectors + position→ Transformer layers→ next-token probabilities→ choose a token & repeat
01 Tokens & conditional probability

An LLM generates a sequence one token at a time. Tokens can be words, parts of words, or punctuation. Each choice depends on the available context and previously generated tokens.

P(x₁, …, xₙ) = ∏ₜ P(xₜ | x₁, …, xₜ₋₁)

Read it: The probability of a sequence is the product of its next-token probabilities. The ∏ symbol means “multiply,” and | means “given.”

Example: After “The invoice total is,” the model predicts a continuation. A likely continuation can still contain an incorrect amount.

Business implication: token probability measures likelihood under the model, not whether a statement is true.

02 Embeddings & vector similarity

Token embeddings map token IDs to learned lists of numbers called vectors. Position information helps represent order. Retrieval systems often use a separate embedding model to represent whole questions and passages.

cosine(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)

Read it: Multiply corresponding coordinates and add them for the dot product a · b. Divide by the two vector lengths to compare direction.

Example: For a = [1, 0] and b = [1, 1], similarity is 1 / √2 ≈ 0.707. This is a geometric score, not a 70.7% chance that a document is correct.

Business implication: related passages may still be outdated, unauthorized, or irrelevant to the exact question.

03 Attention & context

Attention mixes information across token positions. Queries (Q) and keys (K) produce matching scores; values (V) carry the information being combined.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dₖ) V

Read it: Compare queries with keys, scale by the square root of key dimension dₖ, normalize the scores, and combine values. A causal mask blocks future tokens during prediction.

Example: In “The contract expires in June. Renew it,” context helps connect “it” to the contract.

Business implication: attention weights are not a reliable explanation of why an answer is correct.

04 Layers & learned transformations

A Transformer repeats attention and feed-forward transformations, with residual connections and normalization. A simplified feed-forward calculation is:

FFN(h) = W₂ φ(W₁h + b₁) + b₂

Read it: Learned matrices W transform the input vector h, biases b shift it, and a nonlinear function φ lets layers represent more than a single linear mapping.

Example: Successive layers transform the representation of a sentence before the output layer scores possible next tokens.

Business implication: model weights encode learned patterns; they are not a searchable ledger of verified business facts.

05 Training loss & gradient descent

During next-token pretraining, the model learns from known continuations. Cross-entropy penalizes assigning a low probability to the actual next token. Gradient descent adjusts the parameters.

L = −(1/N) ∑ₜ log Pθ(xₜ | x<ₜ)
θ ← θ − η ∇θL

Read it: Average the negative log probabilities over N training targets. θ represents learned parameters, η is the learning rate, and ∇θL points toward increasing loss.

Example: Giving the actual token probability 0.8 produces a smaller penalty than giving it 0.2. Later training may use other objectives to improve instruction following or reasoning.

Business implication: lower prediction loss does not by itself establish fitness for your business task. Test task outcomes separately.

06 Softmax, temperature & sampling

The model outputs scores called logits. Softmax converts scores to a probability distribution; decoding chooses the next token.

pᵢ = exp(zᵢ / T) / ∑ⱼ exp(zⱼ / T), T > 0

Read it: Exponentiate each score z after dividing by temperature T, then divide by the total. The probabilities sum to one.

Example: With two logits [2, 1], probabilities are approximately [0.731, 0.269] at T = 1 and [0.881, 0.119] at T = 0.5. Lower temperature concentrates the distribution.

Business implication: less variation does not guarantee more factual accuracy. Deterministic decoding can repeatedly return the same mistake.

07 Reasoning as intermediate computation

Generating intermediate steps can help a model decompose a problem and use earlier results. Research on chain-of-thought prompting found improvements on several reasoning benchmarks; this is an empirical technique, not a guarantee of sound logic.

Given: 120 units × $25
Subtotal: $3,000
After 10% discount: $3,000 × 0.90 = $2,700

Read it: Each intermediate result becomes an input to the next operation. In a business system, send arithmetic to a calculator and retain inputs and checked results.

Limits: A fluent explanation can contain an invalid step or rationalize a wrong conclusion. A visible explanation is not a complete or necessarily faithful account of internal computation.

Business implication: request a concise justification, evidence, assumptions, and verifiable results; do not treat length as proof of reasoning quality.

08 Verification, multiple attempts & error

Self-consistency samples multiple solution paths and selects a common answer. It improved results in the cited study, but agreement is not independent verification: models can share the same mistaken assumption.

Illustration: P(all 10 steps correct) = 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 0.599

Read it: If ten steps each had 95% accuracy and their errors were independent, all ten would be correct about 60% of the time. Real reasoning errors can be dependent; this is a teaching illustration, not an LLM accuracy estimate.

Example: Three matching refund recommendations still fail if they all use an expired policy. Check against the current policy and an authorized transaction system.

Business implication: use independent checks—source validation, calculations, rule engines, and human review—at consequential steps.

09 Reasoning training & compute budgets

Some reasoning models receive additional training that rewards successful solutions. At inference time, they may spend more computation generating intermediate tokens or exploring alternatives.

J(θ) = E[ R(solution) ]

Read it: A simplified reinforcement learning objective is to increase expected reward R under the model’s generated solutions. Practical algorithms add constraints and other terms; a reward must be designed and checked.

Example: A math task can reward answers verified by a checker. An ambiguous business recommendation rarely has such a clean correctness signal.

Business implication: more reasoning effort can trade higher cost and latency for better task performance, but improvement is not guaranteed. Measure accuracy, cost, and response time on your own cases.
